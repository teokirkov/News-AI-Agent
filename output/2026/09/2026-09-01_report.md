## 1. US and Iran trade strikes over the Strait of Hormuz, ending a month-long pause
*World / Conflict*

American forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz on Aug 30, with US officials saying the launchers were being prepared to fire sea-mine-carrying missiles into the waterway. Iran responded with ballistic missiles aimed at US bases in Jordan — Jordan's military said it destroyed eight before impact — and a drone toward the UAE, which the Emirates said it intercepted over its territorial waters and called a dangerous escalation. It was the first direct military exchange between the two countries in roughly a month, after a pause ordered by President Trump, and it came alongside a separate incident in which a supertanker caught fire after striking naval mines in the strait; Iran's Revolutionary Guard blamed the United States, which denied involvement. Trump told Fox News the US would "hit them hard," and posted an AI-generated video depicting Iran's Kharg Island energy hub being destroyed — an event that did not occur. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian said Tehran does not seek war and would "immediately" reciprocate if Washington honoured an interim agreement reached in June, while Brent crude rose roughly 3.8% to above $91 a barrel and commercial shipping through the strait stayed below half of pre-war levels.

> **Coverage note:** Sources differ on casualties and attribution. Iranian officials reported two people killed in the US strikes, a figure not independently confirmed in other reporting; responsibility for the mines that struck the tanker is disputed between Tehran and Washington, with no independent verification as of publish time.

## 2. Nepal–Tibet flood death toll passes 1,000 with more than 4,400 missing
*World / Disaster*

The disaster that began on Aug 26, when a glacier on Mount Langtang Lirung fractured at roughly 5,200 metres and sent an ice-and-rock avalanche down into the border valleys as a debris flow, has become one of the deadliest natural events of the year. Combined confirmed deaths crossed 1,000 over the weekend — reported as 987 in Nepal and 16 in China — across Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading, Kavre and the Kathmandu Valley on the Nepali side and the Gyirong border crossing in Tibet. Thousands remain unaccounted for, including 639 hydropower workers and hundreds of foreign nationals, with Indian and Chinese citizens the largest groups among them. Rescue teams are still trying to reach cut-off communities, hampered by terrain, destroyed roads and degraded communications, and South Korea has dispatched a 44-member relief team. Attention has also turned to survival prospects for people believed trapped inside hydropower tunnels, which specialists quoted in coverage described as narrowing sharply.

> **Coverage note:** This is an ongoing story that originally broke on Aug 26 and was reported at 157 deaths in last week's edition. Missing-persons figures diverge materially between sources — roughly 4,462 (Anadolu), about 4,200 (CBS News), and 3,916 in Nepal plus 546 in China (Al Jazeera). Treat all counts as provisional.

## 3. White House details $100bn Venezuela oil deal that gives the Pentagon an equity stake
*Business / World*

The administration released terms of an agreement under which Venezuela's interim government, led by Delcy Rodríguez, grants 100-year development rights over 17 oil fields holding roughly 65 billion barrels of proven reserves to North American Blue Energy Partners, a company owned by Venezuelan businessman Alejandro Betancourt. The Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital takes an ownership stake in the venture, which analysts describe as the first time the US government has become an equity investor in an overseas oil project, and the company commits to $100bn in new infrastructure investment. Energy specialists quoted across coverage were sceptical about the timeline: most of the fields are undeveloped, one analyst estimated Venezuela might add only 200,000–300,000 barrels per day next year, and the country's heavy and extra-heavy crude is a poor match for the light oil the Strategic Petroleum Reserve normally holds. Reporting also noted that Betancourt faces money-laundering charges in Spain and Switzerland, and that the deal was struck with a government whose legitimacy is contested, raising the prospect of future renegotiation. Former president Nicolás Maduro, captured in a January military operation on narcoterrorism and drug-trafficking charges, released photographs from US detention during the week.

> **Coverage note:** Sources disagree on what share of output the US secures. Al Jazeera reports a guaranteed right to buy 20% of output at cost through the State Department alongside a 35% Pentagon stake in the venture; PBS NewsHour describes the arrangement as securing 55% of effective output. The discrepancy is unresolved in available reporting.

## 4. DR Congo's Ebola epidemic becomes the fastest-spreading on record
*Health*

The outbreak in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo passed 6,000 confirmed cases during the week, with 6,041 cases, 2,911 deaths and 1,366 recoveries reported by Aug 31 across some 60 affected areas. UN Secretary-General António Guterres described it as the fastest-spreading Ebola epidemic ever recorded, and the WHO has warned it could exceed the 2014–16 West African epidemic, which killed more than 11,000 people. The outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo strain, for which no vaccine or treatment is approved; authorities began a vaccination campaign in Kisangani on Aug 27 using the Ervebo vaccine — licensed only for the Zaire strain — under a compassionate-use programme, starting with health workers across 14 health zones, with more than 50,000 doses delivered. Response efforts are being undermined by armed conflict, population displacement, strikes by health workers over unpaid wages, and flight restrictions disrupting medical supply chains. Separately, Uganda was declared Ebola-free on Aug 28 after 42 days without a new case.

> **Coverage note:** Limited coverage — only 2 credible independent sources were found reporting this story in detail as of publish time, despite its scale, and both are outlets without a lean rating in our reference file. Case and death figures also moved during the week (Africanews reported over 5,700 cases and 2,700 deaths on Aug 28; Al Jazeera reported 6,041 and 2,911 on Aug 31), reflecting the pace of spread rather than a contradiction.

## 6. NASA launches the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope
*Science & Tech*

NASA's next flagship observatory lifted off on Aug 30 aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy from Florida, completing a launch campaign that had been cleared the previous week. Roman carries a wide-field infrared instrument with a field of view at least 100 times larger than Hubble's, designed to survey large areas of sky to study dark energy and dark matter, and to detect and characterise exoplanets. NASA has since confirmed the spacecraft completed its first mid-course correction burn and is on a roughly three-month, million-mile cruise to its operating orbit. The mission is named for Nancy Grace Roman, NASA's first chief astronomer, whose advocacy was central to Hubble.

## 7. Hague tribunal rules India cannot walk away from the Indus Waters Treaty
*World / Politics*

An arbitration tribunal seated at the Permanent Court of Arbitration ruled on Aug 31 that India has no legal basis to suspend or terminate the 1960 Indus Waters Treaty, the World Bank-brokered agreement that allocates the Indus basin's rivers between India and Pakistan and underpins water supply for hundreds of millions of people. The tribunal also issued interim measures directing India to halt work on the Ratle hydroelectric project pending resolution. India rejected the ruling. The treaty has survived multiple wars between the two states, and the decision goes to whether one party can unilaterally exit an agreement the other treats as existential for its agriculture and drinking water.

## 8. FTC and 22 states sue Amazon over alleged advertising overcharges
*Business / Tech*

The Federal Trade Commission, joined by 22 state attorneys general, filed suit on Aug 31 alleging Amazon secretly and systematically overcharged advertisers by manipulating the bidding process in its ad auctions, to the tune of tens of billions of dollars and affecting roughly 1.2 million brands and sellers. Amazon rejected the characterisation, saying the FTC misrepresents how its auctions work and that advertising quality has improved while inflation-adjusted cost-per-click has stayed flat. Amazon's advertising arm generated close to $69bn in revenue in 2025.
